What we found in Parts 1 and 2

Part 1 · August 2026

184 U.S. adults

  • 65% had used AI to look up health information, most often about symptoms and possible diagnoses (81% of those users).
  • 71% vs. 56% Women used AI for health more than men, and more often turned to AI first for emotional support (51% vs. 26%).
  • 59% vs. 47% Men more often told their provider what they had found with AI.

Part 2 · October 2026

64 U.S. adults from our August and September pulses

  • 39% would go to an AI chatbot before any person with a worrying health question; 61% had used AI for emotional support.
  • 70% to 53% For a worrying question, asking family fell from 70% to 53% and a close friend from 56% to 44%; AI fell only from 84% to 78%.
  • 58% of those who would go to AI first said the top reason was asking without feeling like a burden. People still rated their own doctor favorably, including on trust (PDRQ-9).

Part 3A looks inside real AI health chats, starting with what people asked. Part 3B covers what the AI said back, and how people felt afterward.

Online surveys via Prolific. Part 2 re-surveyed people from the earlier pulses.

Now what does that mean?

For research

  • AI chats can be a new source of patient voice. In their follow-ups, 87% of people added detail about their situation, so these transcripts capture symptoms and context in people's own words.
  • Chats show how health problems sound before a diagnosis. People described everyday symptoms across 11 areas of health, most often muscles, bones and joints (20%), and about half never named a condition.

For healthcare & pharma

  • AI is steering people toward care, and toward drugs, usually unprompted. Only 16% asked whether or where to get care, yet the AI pointed 80% of people to a professional. 8 of the 11 people who got a drug recommendation had not asked about any medication, and 10 of the 11 drugs were over the counter.
  • “What is causing this?” is the front door. 46% asked what was causing a symptom, and only 12% asked whether it was serious, which makes symptom explanation the moment where disease education matters most.

Where the risk sits

  • AI rarely hedges, and people rarely push back. Only 10% of AI replies included a disclaimer, and only 4 of 30 people who sent follow-ups questioned an answer.
  • Care decisions often go unasked. 28% hoped AI would help them decide about care, but only 16% asked it outright, and 1 in 10 asked on behalf of someone the AI never heard from.

Notes: exploratory pulse; 50 participants whose pasted conversation included their own messages. Group sizes are small, so differences are directional. BH-adjusted p-values are shown where tests were run. Question types, health areas, who the question was about and follow-up behavior were coded from participant messages; referral, medication, urgent-care and disclaimer codes from the AI text; goals from the survey answers. Chat excerpts are trimmed, with spelling lightly corrected and identifying details left out.
