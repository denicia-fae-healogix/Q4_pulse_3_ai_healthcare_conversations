Now what does that mean?

For research

  • AI chats can be a new source of patient voice. In their follow-ups, 87% of people who kept chatting added detail about their situation, so these transcripts capture symptoms and context in people's own words.
  • Chats show how people describe health problems in their own words. People described everyday symptoms across 10 areas of health, most often muscles, bones and joints (20%).

For healthcare & pharma

  • AI is steering people toward care, and sometimes toward OTC drugs, usually unprompted. Only 16% asked whether or where to get care, yet the AI pointed 80% of people to a professional. 8 of the 11 people who got a drug recommendation had not asked about any medication, and 10 of the 11 drugs were over the counter.
  • “What is causing this?” is the front door. 46% asked what was causing a symptom, and only 12% asked whether it was serious. Cause questions are also where the AI referred most reliably, so this is the moment that most shapes whether people reach an HCP.

Where the risk sits

  • AI rarely adds a disclaimer, and people rarely push back. Only 10% of AI replies included a disclaimer, and only 4 of 30 people who sent follow-ups questioned an answer.
  • Care decisions often go unasked. 28% hoped AI would help them decide about care, but only 16% asked it outright. People who asked about their goal directly tended to say it was met more often (91% vs. 64%), and 1 in 10 asked on behalf of someone the AI never heard from.

Notes: exploratory pulse; 50 participants whose pasted conversation included their own messages. Group sizes are small, so differences are directional. BH-adjusted p-values are shown where tests were run. Question types, health areas, who the question was about and follow-up behavior were coded from participant messages; referral, medication, urgent-care and disclaimer codes from the AI text; goals from the survey answers.
