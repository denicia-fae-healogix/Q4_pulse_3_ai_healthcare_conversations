What we found in Parts 1 and 2

Part 1 · August 2026

184 U.S. adults

  • 65% had used AI to look up health information, most often about symptoms and possible diagnoses (81% of those users).
  • 71% vs. 56% Women used AI for health more than men, and more often turned to AI first for emotional support (51% vs. 26%).
  • 59% vs. 47% Men more often told their provider what they had found with AI.

Part 2 · October 2026

64 U.S. adults from our August and September pulses

  • 39% would go to an AI chatbot before any person with a worrying health question; 61% had used AI for emotional support.
  • 70% to 53% For a worrying question, asking family fell from 70% to 53% and a close friend from 56% to 44%; AI fell only from 84% to 78%.
  • 58% of those who would go to AI first said the top reason was asking without feeling like a burden. People still rated their own doctor favorably, including on trust (PDRQ-9).

Part 3A looked at what people asked AI. Part 3B looks at what the AI said back, and how people felt afterward.

Now what does that mean?

For research

  • Code what the AI said, not just what people asked. Referral, urgent-care and medication advice each lined up with different emotional outcomes.
  • Measure feelings before and after the chat; distress fell on average (18.5 to 15.2), while positive feelings stayed flat.

For healthcare & pharma

  • An AI referral may calm people, and it sticks. Referred participants felt less distressed afterward, and 58% recalled a clear recommendation (vs. 9%).
  • Medication suggestions did not reassure. No one who got a medication recommendation felt calmer, so expect these patients to arrive with questions.

Where the risk sits

  • AI referrals reached the most distressed. People the AI referred started out more distressed (19.7 vs. 14.4) and dropped further by the end of the chat.
  • Short chats left people no better off. One to three messages ended less positive; four or more ended about 3 points more positive.

Notes: exploratory pulse; 57 participants with complete before and after PANAS scores. Group sizes are small, so group differences are directional. BH-adjusted p-values are shown where tests were run. Conversation codes (referral, urgent care, medication, personal details) were coded from the participant and AI text.
